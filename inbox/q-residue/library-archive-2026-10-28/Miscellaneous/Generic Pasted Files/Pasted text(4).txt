The brief asks for playable inference from incomplete language-like pieces while forbidding semantic completion, translation, or cultural authority. PLAUSIBLE PLAY FORMS
All entries below are DESIGN PROPOSAL unless otherwise marked.

1. Restoration draft
Players place a [BLANK] beside or under a piece they suspect is incomplete. They may write a proposed mark on the blank.
Physical operations: adjacency, writing, covering, distance.
Evidence anchor: supplied omission fact + supplied blanks.
Status: MECHANICAL INFERENCE that blanks can carry restoration proposals; DESIGN PROPOSAL for the actual rule.

2. Visible-edge chain
Players arrange pieces so touching edges share an exact visible string, such as SARILI, LOO, ISI, or NA.
Physical operations: adjacency, orientation, reversal, distance.
Evidence anchor: repeated visible strings.
Status: MECHANICAL INFERENCE for matching possibility; DESIGN PROPOSAL for the rule.

3. Connector bridge
Players use short pieces A, NA, SA, NI between larger pieces to make or break connections.
Physical operations: adjacency, distance, orientation.
Evidence anchor: presence of short pieces and repeated NA inside longer pieces.
Status: DESIGN PROPOSAL.

4. Substring harvest
Players collect or stack pieces that share a visible substring, such as SARILI, LOO, ISI, or BASTOS.
Physical operations: stacking, grouping, overlap.
Evidence anchor: repeated strings and duplicate BASTOS.
Status: DESIGN PROPOSAL.

5. Silent response probe
One piece is placed as a prompt. Other players register acceptance, uncertainty, refusal, or correction by distance, covering, blank placement, or silence.
Physical operations: distance, covering, silence, writing on blanks.
Evidence anchor: evocative phrase pieces, blanks, and the supplied fact that omission can be recognized after a cue.
Status: DESIGN PROPOSAL.

6. Hidden-consonant bluff
One player secretly marks a blank with a proposed restoration. Other players guess whether the blank is a serious restoration, a wildcard, or a decoy.
Physical operations: covering, stacking, adjacency, reveal.
Evidence anchor: omission fact, blanks, duplicate BASTOS as possible decoy.
Status: DESIGN PROPOSAL.

ADDITIONAL ORDINARY PIECES DECISION
Decision: Yes, additional ordinary items are necessary for the five-minute physical test, but only as test equipment, not as game content.

Necessary ordinary item 1: one pencil or erasable marker.
Job: allow a player to mark [BLANK] pieces without permanently destroying supplied pieces.
Mechanical reason: without a writing tool, the blank-writing function cannot be tested.

Necessary ordinary item 2: one cue slip.
Job: deliver the one small cue and the basic task without a spoken lecture.
Candidate non-Tagalog wording, unvalidated as game text:
“Some pieces may be missing an ending. Make any silent arrangement. There is no correct answer. Use blanks only if needed.”
Back of slip:
“Point to the part you are least sure about.”

Optional ordinary item: observer timer.
Job: enforce five-minute limit.
Status: not a game component.

No Tagalog wording is proposed. If language bias is a concern, the cue slip can be replaced by a nonverbal gap-symbol card showing a visible empty final slot and an arrow toward a blank. The practical job is the same: cue possible omission without lecturing.

FAILURE MODES
Most items below are MECHANICAL INFERENCE.

1. Objective misunderstanding.
The player may ask, “What am I supposed to make?” because the pieces feel like meaningful language but no goal is given.

2. Profound-answer pressure.
The player may feel forced to produce a correct sentence, poem, moral statement, or culturally meaningful arrangement.

3. Translation freeze.
The player may stop manipulating pieces and request or seek translation.

4. Completion compulsion.
The player may silently restore omitted sounds and treat the restoration as fact.

5. Blank paralysis.
The player may refuse to write on [BLANK] pieces for fear of defacing components or being wrong.

6. Cue overfit.
After hearing “missing ending,” the player may assume every piece is incomplete.

7. Expertise asymmetry.
A player who knows Tagalog or another related language may feel pressured to become an authority, or may be treated as one by others.

8. Duplicate confusion.
The two BASTOS pieces may be read as a mistake, a joke, an offense, or a hidden clue.

9. Model contamination.
Players may ask a machine for completions, which would test model fluency rather than human physical play.

10. Observer bias.
The observer may interpret arbitrary arrangement as grammar, spirituality, or narrative when the evidence is only placement.

MINIMUM FIVE-MINUTE PROTOTYPE
Purpose: observe what a human naturally does with a small subset of language-like pieces after one small omission cue.

Players:
1 builder + 1 observer.

Components required:
Six supplied phrase pieces:

MATUWID NA ISI
MALINI NA LOO
MARANGA NA LOO

Two supplied short pieces:
A
NA

Two supplied blank pieces:
[BLANK]
[BLANK]

One ordinary pencil.
One ordinary cue slip.
One ordinary observer timer, optional.

Cue slip front:
“Some pieces may be missing an ending. Make any silent arrangement. There is no correct answer. Use blanks only if needed.”

Cue slip back:
“Point to the part you are least sure about.”

Setup:
1. Place the ten supplied game pieces face up in a loose random cluster.
2. Give the builder the pencil and cue slip.
3. The observer does not speak.
4. The observer starts the timer when the builder receives the cue slip.

Procedure:
1. The builder has 4 minutes to arrange the pieces silently.
2. The builder may move, rotate, reverse, stack, overlap, cover, separate, leave gaps, or write on blanks.
3. If the builder speaks, the observer points to the cue slip and remains silent.
4. At 4 minutes, the observer shows the back of the cue slip.
5. The builder points to the part they are least sure about.
6. Total elapsed time: no more than 5 minutes.

Removed for this test:
No scoring.
No win condition.
No turn order.
No translation.
No spoken explanation.
No additional pieces.
No cultural framing.
No authoritative language rule.

FIRST MECHANICAL FAILURE YOU EXPECT
The builder will treat the fragments as requiring a correct linguistic, poetic, or culturally profound answer. The likely first failure is a legitimacy freeze: the builder pauses, asks whether the arrangement is “correct,” asks for translation, or waits for permission, instead of physically testing adjacency, omission, blanks, or connectors.

ONE QUESTION THE PHYSICAL TEST CAN ANSWER BETTER THAN A MODEL
After one small omission cue, does the human first respond to suspected missing endings by writing on or placing a [BLANK], or do they ignore blanks and arrange only by visible repeated strings?
