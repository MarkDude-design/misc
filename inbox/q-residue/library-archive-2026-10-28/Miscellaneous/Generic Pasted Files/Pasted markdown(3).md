Etymology of the word regicide

**Takeaway:**   **Regicide** comes from **Latin** ***rēgis*** **(“of a king”) +** ***-cide*** **(“killer” or “killing”)**, entering English in the **1540s** to mean *a person who kills a king*, and by **c.1600** also *the act of killing a king*. 

## 🏛️ **Core Etymology**

- **Latin** ***rēgis*** — genitive of *rex*, meaning “king.” This root appears across many monarchy‑related English words (regal, regent, reign). 
- **Latin** ***‑cide*** — from *-cida* (“killer”) and *-cidium* (“killing”), ultimately from *caedere* (“to cut/strike”). It’s the same element in homicide, patricide, tyrannicide. 

Put together, **regi‑ + cide** literally means **“king‑killing.”**

## 📜 **Earliest Uses**

- **1540s** — first attested meaning: *“a king‑killer.”* 
- **1548** — earliest OED evidence. 
- **c.1600** — meaning expands to *“the killing of a king.”* 

Wiktionary confirms it as a **learned borrowing from Medieval Latin** ***rēgicidium*** (“king‑killing”). 

## 🗡️ **Two Related Latin Forms**

- **rēgicidium** — “king‑killing” → the *act*
- **rēgicida** — “king‑killer” → the *person*   English inherited **both senses**, which is why *regicide* can mean *either the act or the perpetrator*. 

## 🧭 **Why This Word Appears in Political History**

Because *rex* comes from the Proto‑Indo‑European root *\\*reg‑\*, meaning “to rule, to direct,” the term *regicide* sits in the same linguistic family as *reign*, *regal*, *regimen*, *regular*, and even *right* (via “straight, direct”).
