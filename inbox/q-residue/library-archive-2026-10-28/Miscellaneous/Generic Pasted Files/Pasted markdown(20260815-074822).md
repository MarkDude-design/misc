Are there any series on why in  Tagalog bones and seeds are buto, but fish has tinik?

Short answer: **No known documentary “series” or formal linguistic program** specifically tackles *why* Tagalog uses **buto** for bones/seeds but **tinik** for fish bones. But we *can* explain the phenomenon clearly — and the search results confirm that the distinction is lexical, cultural, and metaphorical rather than anatomical.

## 🧠 **The actual linguistic explanation (based on what we** ***do*** **know)**

### **1.** ***Buto*** **= anything that is a “core hard thing inside”**

Search results show *buto* means **bone** and **seed** — the shared idea is “a hard internal structure.”    This is common in Austronesian languages: the same root often covers bones, seeds, kernels, pits.

### **2.** ***Tinik*** **= “thorn” → extended metaphor for fish bones**

*Tinik* literally means **thorn** or **spine**, and only *secondarily* “fish bone.”    This tells us something important:

> Fish bones are conceptualized not as “bones” but as **thorns** — sharp, thin, piercing hazards.

This metaphor is ancient and widespread in Philippine languages.

### **3. Why the split? (Inference based on sources + linguistic patterns)**

No source directly answers the question, but the pattern is clear:

- **Mammal bones** → thick, structural → *buto*
- **Seeds** → hard inner core → *buto*
- **Fish bones** → thin, needle-like, dangerous → *tinik* (“thorn”)

So Tagalog categorizes based on **shape + danger**, not biology.

This is consistent with how other Philippine languages extend *tinik* to mean:

- splinter
- prickle
- thorn
- sharp spine 

Fish bones fit the *tinik* category perfectly.

## 📚 **Are there academic works that touch this indirectly?**

There is **no series** on this specific lexical contrast, but there *are* works on Tagalog body-part metaphors and indigenous conceptual systems — for example, the study on *buto* as a culturally loaded concept in folk narratives.

But none of them discuss *tinik* vs *buto* directly.

## 🧩 **So what’s the real answer?**

**Because Tagalog categorizes by** ***function and shape*****, not by anatomical correctness.**

- Hard internal core → *buto*
- Sharp, piercing, thorn-like → *tinik*

Fish bones are treated as **hazards**, not “bones,” so they get the “thorn” word.

Proto Austronesian bone terms

**Short answer:**   Proto‑Austronesian (PAN) *did* have a reconstructable “bone” root — *\\*cuRut\* — and it did **not** use the ancestor of *buto* for “bone.” The modern Tagalog *buto* (“bone/seed”) comes from a **different PAN root**, one that originally meant **testicle** (*\\*buCuq\*), not “bone.” This is why the Tagalog semantic field looks so strange today.

Below is the full, structured breakdown with citations.

# 🧠 The Proto‑Austronesian bone root

The standard reconstruction for **“bone”** in Proto‑Austronesian is:

- *\\*cuRut\* = “bone” (attested in comparative reconstructions such as Dempwolff, Blust, and the Austronesian Comparative Dictionary) 

This root survives cleanly in many Formosan and Malayo‑Polynesian languages, but **not** in Tagalog.

# 🧬 The Proto‑Austronesian ancestor of *buto*

The Tagalog word **buto** (“bone; seed”) does **not** come from the PAN bone root. Instead, Wiktionary’s etymology shows:

- **Tagalog** ***buto*** ← Proto‑Philippine *\\*butuq\*
- ← Proto‑Malayo‑Polynesian *\\*butuq\*
- ← Proto‑Austronesian *\\*buCuq\* = “testicle” 

This is extremely well‑attested across Philippine and Malayic languages:

- Ilocano *buto* = penis
- Hiligaynon *buto* = penis
- Malay *butuh* = penis
- Malagasy *voto* = penis
- Saisiyat *bosoe’* = testicle

**Inference:**   The semantic shift from “testicle” → “seed” → “pit” → “bone” is a **metaphorical chain** based on “small hard internal core.” This is a common semantic pathway in Austronesian languages.

# 🐟 What about *tinik*?

PAN also had a root for **thorn/spine**:

- *\\*tiNiS / \tiNiq\*\* (reconstructed “thorn, spine”) → Tagalog **tinik** (“thorn; fish bone”)

Fish bones are universally conceptualized as **thorns**, not “bones,” in many Austronesian languages — which is why *tinik* is used instead of *buto*.

# 🧩 Putting it together: Why Tagalog looks “weird” today

### 1. The *real* PAN bone word (\\\*cuRut) did **not** survive in Tagalog.

It was replaced by a metaphorical extension of *buto*.

### 2. *Buto* originally meant “testicle,” not “bone.”

The shift to “seed” and “bone” is a later innovation.

### 3. Fish bones are categorized as “thorns,” not “bones.”

This is inherited from the PAN thorn root, not the bone root.
